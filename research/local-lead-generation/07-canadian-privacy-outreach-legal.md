# Report 7 — Canadian Privacy, Outreach, Consumer Protection and Legal Structure

**Current as of:** 2026-07-15  
**Geographic scope:** Ottawa, Ontario, Canada  
**Decision context:** OAS pilot for Ottawa backflow testing/repair leads  
**Important:** This is business research, not legal or tax advice. An Ontario lawyer and accountant should review the live customer flow and contracts before launch.

## Executive conclusion

The model is viable only if OAS is transparent about who it is, who will perform the work, and what happens to a prospect's information. The safest first version is **not** a fictional local contractor and **not** OAS acting as an unlicensed prime contractor. It is one of:

1. OAS operating as the genuine service business, with Alex/OAS performing, contracting, invoicing and warranting the represented work;
2. an OAS-owned, clearly labelled referral site that tells the customer their information will be sent to one named, qualified Ottawa provider; or
3. a marketing site licensed to one real provider, using that provider's accurate identity while the provider controls service delivery, customer contracts, invoices and any eligible Google Business Profile.

If Alex will personally perform the pilot work and all current qualification, insurance, equipment, reporting, tax and WSIB requirements are met, the direct-operator structure creates the strongest first-party learning. For a partner-operated pilot, the exclusive-provider structure is operationally cleaner. A transparent referral structure preserves more OAS asset independence, but it requires more deliberate consent language and clearer allocation of responsibility. OAS may own the underlying domain, code and analytics account; the actual contractor should own the service promise, quote, job, warranty and eligible Business Profile. If that profile links to an OAS-owned site or number, the provider must have genuine contractual control over those authoritative assets for as long as they represent the provider. If OAS wants a provider-portable referral asset, keep it clearly independent and profile-free.

The legal load-bearing points are:

- **PIPEDA applies.** Ontario has no generally applicable private-sector privacy law deemed substantially similar to PIPEDA. A lead form, tracked call, recording, CRM record and disclosure to a contractor can all involve personal information collected, used or disclosed in commercial activity.
- **Consent must be meaningful at the point of collection.** Burying lead sharing in a privacy policy is not enough. The person should understand what is collected, why, and which provider—or clearly described class of providers—will receive it.
- **Business call recording needs notice and purpose disclosure.** Canada's Criminal Code permits interception with the consent of one party, but the Office of the Privacy Commissioner says a business recording customer calls should inform the customer, state the purpose and obtain consent under PIPEDA. The criminal-law exception is not a substitute for privacy compliance.
- **Cold outreach is regulated.** CASL covers commercial email and text. CRTC telemarketing rules cover unsolicited solicitation calls. Business-to-business calls are exempt from the National DNCL screening rules, but telemarketing identification, internal do-not-call and registration obligations can still apply.
- **The public-facing identity cannot create a false general impression.** Federal Competition Act and Ontario consumer-protection rules reach materially false or misleading representations. A site that appears to be the service company while silently auctioning or reselling the inquiry creates avoidable risk.
- **Backflow work is credential-sensitive.** Ottawa's current Water By-law identifies who may survey, install, replace, test or repair devices. Plumbing is a compulsory Ontario trade, and a provider's status can be checked in the Skilled Trades Ontario public register. OAS should not represent that a person is qualified until the exact task and current credential have been verified.

**Overall conclusion: Proceed with conditions.** Launch only after the disclosure, consent, privacy program, chosen responsibility structure, insurance/credential checks and customer flow have passed appropriate legal, accounting and broker review; a provider agreement and handoff design are additionally required for Models A, B or D. Confidence is **high** on the regulatory principles and **medium** on how a regulator or court would characterize a particular OAS flow because that depends on exact wording, control and commercial reality.

## What the episode proposes—and what it omits

The episode describes owning sites and tracking numbers, routing inquiries to local contractors, and charging rent or revenue share. It also discusses creating the appearance of another local competitor and using contractor assistance for map listings. Those are business claims and tactics, not evidence that the arrangement complies with Canadian law, Google policy or a customer's reasonable expectations.

The episode does not resolve:

- whether the consumer thinks they are contacting the site owner, the contractor or a referral marketplace;
- which party controls the lead record and answers access/deletion requests;
- whether calls are recorded and how notice is given;
- whether customer information may be sent to one provider, several providers or later buyers;
- who is liable for misleading content, missed appointments, defective work or property damage;
- whether the contractor's licences, backflow qualifications, insurance and WSIB position are current;
- CASL, telemarketing, tax, breach-response or retention obligations; or
- how responsibility changes if OAS takes payment, sets prices, promises outcomes or directs the work.

These are not paperwork details. They determine whether OAS is a publisher/referrer, an agent, a joint service operator or the contracting supplier.

## 1. Choose the legal and customer-facing model first

### Model A — transparent referral or matching service

The page says, in substance, “OAS helps Ottawa property owners request backflow service from a qualified local provider.” The form names the provider before submission where possible and says which contact and job details will be shared so the provider can quote or schedule. The contractor contacts the prospect, contracts directly, performs and invoices.

**Advantages:** OAS can retain domain and lead-asset ownership; the consumer receives an honest explanation; providers can change with updated notice and consent.  
**Risks:** OAS must administer consent and privacy requests; a broad “partners may contact you” notice may not be meaningful; sending the same lead to several providers changes the customer's expected experience and should be made prominent.

### Model B — exclusive provider site licensed by OAS

The website accurately presents the real provider. The provider approves all credential, pricing, warranty and service claims; receives all inquiries; contracts and invoices directly; and controls its legitimate Google Business Profile. OAS licenses the site/number or provides marketing services under a written agreement that gives the provider genuine control of any website and phone presented as its authoritative assets.

**Advantages:** the public identity and service operator align; one-provider consent is simpler; GBP eligibility rests with the real-world business.  
**Risks:** switching providers requires a full identity/content/consent change; the provider may claim goodwill or phone-number rights; OAS must avoid making the site look stable while secretly changing the performing business.

### Model C — OAS as the direct service operator

OAS accurately presents itself as the service business; Alex/OAS performs the represented work, contracts with the customer, invoices, keeps the service records and owns the warranty obligation. This is distinct from selling work that an undisclosed third party performs. Before launch, confirm the current task-specific Ottawa/OWWA qualifications, calibrated equipment and reporting access; obtain field-service CGL and any E&O/cyber coverage the broker recommends; document the WSIB position; and have the accountant confirm HST and revenue treatment. OAS then owns the customer relationship and may use a genuine operator GBP only if the real-world operating facts meet Google's rules.

**Advantages:** first-party job costs, conversion data and customer language; aligned public identity; direct control of service quality.  
**Risks:** OAS bears field safety, workmanship, property-damage, warranty, complaint, privacy, tax and service-capacity obligations. Alex's historical experience or credential does not establish that every launch requirement is current.

### Model D — OAS as prime contractor using subcontractors

OAS quotes or sells the job, collects payment and subcontracts performance. This gives OAS more control and margin but also makes OAS more likely to be treated as the supplier or construction principal. Licensing, warranties, consumer contracts, WSIB clearances, tax, insurance and workmanship liability become more direct.

**Recommendation:** Use Model C only if Alex intends to self-perform and every current operating gate is documented. Do not use Model D for the first pilot. If OAS later wants the subcontracting model, obtain a specific legal opinion, commercial general liability coverage appropriate to the work, and a documented licensing/WSIB structure first.

## 2. Privacy design under PIPEDA

The Office of the Privacy Commissioner (OPC) states that PIPEDA applies to private-sector organizations collecting, using or disclosing personal information in commercial activity. Its ten fair-information principles require accountability, identified purposes, consent, limited collection/use/retention, accuracy, safeguards, openness, individual access and a complaint process.

### Minimum data map

Before building the form, document each data element and its full lifecycle:

| Data | Purpose | Recipient | Proposed retention | Sensitivity/control |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Name and business/property name | Respond to request | OAS and named provider | Active quote period plus defined dispute/accounting window | Role-based CRM access |
| Phone/email | Contact and schedule | OAS, named provider, call-routing vendor | Same as above; marketing consent stored separately | MFA; export/delete procedure |
| Property address/postal code | Confirm Ottawa coverage and dispatch | Named provider; OAS only if necessary | Delete when no longer needed for attribution/dispute | Limit collection before qualification |
| Device/service details | Qualify job | Named provider | Job/quote lifecycle | Avoid unrelated building/tenant details |
| Call metadata | Attribution and quality control | OAS, provider, call vendor | Short, stated analytics window | Restrict raw-number access |
| Call audio, if enabled | Stated QA/attribution purpose only | Strictly limited staff/provider | Shorter than lead record unless justified | Notice, consent, encryption, access log |
| Outcome/revenue | Validate lead economics | OAS and provider | Contract/accounting period | Prefer job ID and amount over customer detail |

This table is a recommended design, not a statutory retention schedule. PIPEDA does not provide one universal period. The OPC says information should be disposed of when the identified purpose is fulfilled unless law requires retention, and organizations should establish minimum and maximum periods.

### Point-of-collection notice

Put the key facts adjacent to the submit button, not only in the footer. A counsel-reviewed starting pattern is:

> By submitting, you ask Ottawa Agent Systems to share the contact and service details above with **[Provider legal/operating name]** so it can contact you about this request. OAS and the provider handle your information as described in the Privacy Notice. This is not consent to unrelated marketing.

If the provider is not known at submission, the page should explain the selection method, maximum number and category of recipients. For example, “one qualified Ottawa backflow provider selected by OAS” is more intelligible than “trusted partners.” If more than one provider will compete for the lead, say so prominently before submission.

A separate, unchecked opt-in should govern newsletters or unrelated offers. Keep the request-for-service consent distinct from optional marketing consent. Store notice version, timestamp, source URL, provider identity and the user's affirmative action so OAS can prove what was agreed to.

### Privacy-management controls

Before collection begins, OAS should:

- name a privacy lead and publish a working contact channel;
- maintain the data inventory, purposes, vendors, storage regions and retention schedule;
- execute a data-handling schedule with the contractor and processor terms with call/CRM/form vendors;
- limit each party to authorized purposes and prohibit resale or unrelated marketing;
- define which party answers access, correction, withdrawal and complaint requests;
- use MFA, least privilege, encrypted transport/storage, offboarding and periodic access review;
- record every security-safeguard breach, even if it does not meet the reporting threshold;
- assess whether a breach creates a real risk of significant harm and, if so, report to the OPC and notify affected individuals as soon as feasible; and
- keep breach records for at least two years, as the OPC's PIPEDA guidance requires.

The principal organization remains accountable for information transferred to a processor and needs sufficient contractual protections. If OAS and a contractor each use lead data for their own purposes, the roles may be more complex than controller/processor terminology suggests; counsel should allocate obligations based on actual control, not contract labels alone.

### Call recording

The Criminal Code's section 184 saving provision allows interception with express or implied consent of the originator or intended recipient. For commercial customer recording, however, OPC guidance is more demanding: state that the call is recorded, clearly state why, seek consent, offer a reasonable alternative when a caller objects, restrict use to the stated purpose, safeguard the file and limit retention.

For the pilot, the lower-risk default is **call tracking without audio recording**. Measure source, duration, answer status and disposition in the CRM. Enable audio only after counsel approves a just-in-time notice and a genuine operational need. Never announce “quality assurance” if recordings are actually used for sales scoring, partner disputes or profiling.

## 3. Outreach: inbound consent, partner prospecting and follow-up

### Consumer callbacks

The CRTC says a consumer callback request submitted through an online form would normally be express consent to be contacted under the National DNCL rules. Make the requested party and purpose clear and preserve proof. Do not treat one backflow request as permission for indefinite promotions or sale to unrelated providers.

### Cold calls to prospective contractor partners

Calls to businesses are exempt from Part II National DNCL screening, but CRTC guidance says all telemarketers must register, even when calls are exempt. Telemarketing rules such as caller identification and an internal do-not-call list remain relevant. Use a real OAS caller ID, identify OAS and the commercial purpose, honour opt-outs, keep a suppression list, and avoid automated announcing devices.

### Cold email or text

CASL generally requires consent, identification information and an unsubscribe mechanism for commercial electronic messages. Implied consent from a conspicuously published business address is fact-specific: the address must have been published by the recipient/account holder, have no no-solicitation statement, and the message must relate to that person's business role. A scraped third-party directory is not automatically a licence to email.

For partner recruitment:

- prefer warm introductions, a website contact form intended for business inquiries, or a carefully documented role-relevant address;
- log the source URL, date, why implied consent applies and any restriction;
- identify OAS and anyone on whose behalf the message is sent;
- provide current mailing/contact information and a functioning unsubscribe mechanism;
- process unsubscribe requests within the CASL period and keep a suppression record; and
- do not buy or scrape bulk lists for a VA campaign.

An inquiry can create time-limited implied consent for relevant follow-up, but OAS should not turn a service request into a general marketing list. The sender bears the burden of proving consent.

## 4. Truthful identity and consumer protection

The Competition Bureau explains that the Competition Act tests both literal meaning and the **general impression** of a representation. Ontario's currently operative Consumer Protection Act, 2002 also prohibits false, misleading or deceptive representations. Ontario's Consumer Protection Act, 2023 was still not in force on the report date, so OAS should monitor its proclamation and implementing regulations.

Practical rules for every page, ad, recorded greeting and form:

- do not imply OAS employs the tester if it does not;
- do not use a fabricated address, team, years-in-business figure, licence number, review or service fleet;
- identify whether prices are the provider's estimate, a range or merely illustrative;
- name who gives the quote, performs the work, invoices, warrants and handles complaints;
- do not state “certified,” “licensed,” “City approved,” “best,” “same-day” or “24/7” without current evidence and operational capacity;
- do not reuse one provider's reviews after routing to another provider;
- disclose referral compensation where its omission could affect the overall impression or customer decision, as counsel advises; and
- keep a dated substantiation file for every material credential, safety, savings, response-time and performance claim.

The episode's “false competitor” or “illusion of saturation” framing should be rejected. It conflicts with the transparent structure recommended here and compounds Google-profile risk discussed in Report 3.

## 5. Backflow-provider verification and allocation of liability

Ottawa's Water By-law No. 2025-227 and its qualified-person materials govern who may perform different backflow tasks. Skilled Trades Ontario says plumber is a compulsory trade: a person must have the applicable valid status and appear in the public register, subject to defined exceptions. OWWA certification may also be required for relevant cross-connection/testing work under the municipal rules. Verify the exact task—not just a company logo—before publishing a claim or routing a job.

The provider file should contain:

- legal and operating name, corporation/business-number details and service address;
- current Skilled Trades Ontario public-register result for each person doing compulsory work;
- current OWWA/cross-connection credential appropriate to survey, test, install or repair;
- City of Ottawa qualified-person/contractor evidence for the exact activity;
- commercial general liability certificate and direct broker verification;
- current WSIB clearance or documented determination of why one is not required;
- privacy, call-handling and complaint procedures;
- after-hours/overflow capacity and geographic limits; and
- monthly re-attestation plus automated expiry reminders.

WSIB states that a principal directly retaining a contractor for construction work must obtain and maintain a valid clearance, subject to specific exceptions. If OAS merely introduces a customer who contracts directly, the analysis may differ. If OAS awards the work or subcontracts it, the principal rules become more likely to apply. Counsel should decide this from the actual contracting and payment flow.

### Provider agreement clauses for legal review

The agreement should address:

- independent businesses and no authority to bind the other party;
- customer-facing identity and which party is the supplier;
- required credentials, insurance, WSIB, permits and immediate notice of lapse;
- permitted services, territory, hours and capacity;
- accurate claims, quote practices, warranties and complaint ownership;
- privacy roles, permitted data use, safeguards, subprocessors, retention, deletion, access requests and breach notice;
- call recording and outreach restrictions;
- lead acceptance/rejection criteria and no resale;
- indemnities and liability caps appropriate to each party's control, without assuming clauses override consumer law;
- domain, code, content, tracking-number, analytics and customer-record ownership;
- termination, transition notices, number routing and deletion/return of data; and
- audit evidence for qualifications, lead outcomes and revenue where compensation depends on them.

Do not rely on “contractor is responsible for everything.” OAS remains responsible for its own advertising, collection, security and representations, and regulators look at actual conduct.

## 6. Tax and entity questions

OAS should have an accountant map each money flow: site rent/licence fee, per-lead fee, referral commission, revenue share, consumer payment and provider payout. The invoice description and tax treatment should match the real supply.

CRA's June 2026 guidance says a business generally loses small-supplier status when worldwide taxable supplies exceed $30,000 under the single-quarter or rolling-four-quarter tests; registration timing differs by test. Ontario taxable supplies generally attract 13% HST under place-of-supply rules. Voluntary registration may be possible earlier. These are not reasons to select a model; they are bookkeeping and pricing inputs requiring accountant confirmation.

If VAs or operators are added, written “independent contractor” wording does not decide worker status. CRA looks at the real relationship. This matters later, not for a founder-run pilot, but the operating model should not assume all delegated labour is automatically self-employed.

## 7. Recommended pilot controls

### Before the site can collect a real lead

1. Select Model A, B or C in a one-page responsibility matrix; exclude Model D unless counsel, the broker and accountant approve its exact facts.
2. Have Ontario counsel review the page identity, form notice, privacy notice, terms and provider agreement.
3. Complete the provider credential, insurance and WSIB file.
4. Name the privacy lead and approve the data map, retention schedule and incident process.
5. Configure form/call vendors with MFA and least privilege; disable call audio by default.
6. Store consent evidence: page/version, wording, provider, timestamp, source and action.
7. Test a privacy access/correction/deletion request end to end.
8. Test a provider termination: stop routing, change disclosures, preserve required records and delete unnecessary copies.
9. Register for National DNCL compliance if OAS will make telemarketing calls, including exempt B2B calls; implement an internal do-not-call list.
10. Ask the accountant to confirm entity, invoicing and GST/HST treatment before the first provider invoice.

### Practical exercise

Run a tabletop using one fictitious lead:

- The person reads the page, calls, declines recording, submits a form, receives one provider callback, asks which parties hold their data, then withdraws marketing consent and requests deletion.
- The provider later reports a CRM export sent to the wrong address and loses its backflow credential.
- OAS must identify the exact disclosure shown, all data locations, who contains and records the incident, whether the RROSH threshold is met, what can legally be deleted, how routing is stopped, and how live claims are removed.

The exercise passes only if every action has a named owner, evidence location and time target.

## Completion test

This knowledge gap is closed enough for a pilot when Alex can produce and explain:

- a counsel-approved model and public identity;
- a complete data-flow and privacy-role diagram;
- point-of-collection and call notices tied to stored consent evidence;
- a PIPEDA privacy program with access, correction, complaint, retention and breach procedures;
- a CASL/telemarketing outreach SOP and suppression records;
- a verified provider file for each offered task;
- a signed agreement allocating service, data, claim and termination responsibilities;
- accountant-confirmed invoice/HST treatment; and
- successful privacy, breach, credential-lapse and provider-exit tabletop tests.

## Failure modes and stop conditions

Stop launch or routing if any of these occurs:

- the page's apparent provider differs from the actual provider and the difference is not made clear;
- a form shares data with an unnamed or unlimited group of providers;
- consent wording or vendor logs cannot prove the disclosure shown at collection;
- a contractor credential, insurance certificate or required WSIB clearance is missing/expired;
- OAS takes consumer payment or directs regulated work without a reviewed prime-contractor structure;
- a partner uses the lead for unrelated marketing or resells it;
- a recorded call lacks the approved notice/purpose or there is no alternative when a caller objects;
- a cold-email source/consent basis cannot be documented;
- a privacy incident has no owner or cannot be contained; or
- a material service/credential claim lacks current substantiation.

## Material unknowns for counsel or regulator confirmation

- Exact characterization of OAS and the provider under the selected contract, payment and control flow.
- Whether a particular backflow test, survey, installation or repair requires each City/OWWA/trade credential; Ottawa's by-law table should be applied task by task.
- Whether referral-fee wording or disclosure is required in the selected service relationship.
- WSIB principal/clearance obligations if OAS routes, schedules, prices or pays for construction work.
- Insurance types and limits for a referral publisher, direct field-service operator and prime contractor.
- Appropriate retention periods for call metadata, audio, lead records, consent proof, disputes and tax records.
- Whether any vendor's cross-border processing or AI features create additional notice, contractual or consent requirements.
- Transition requirements when Ontario's Consumer Protection Act, 2023 is proclaimed.
- Impact of federal Bill C-36 if enacted. As of 2026-07-15 it was only at second reading; PIPEDA remained operative.

## Confidence summary

- **High confidence:** PIPEDA applicability and principles; meaningful-consent requirements; OPC call-recording guidance; CASL and telemarketing baseline; deceptive-marketing principle; current status of Ontario CPA 2023 and federal Bill C-36; compulsory-trade verification.
- **Medium confidence:** Model C is the strongest learning pilot if Alex self-performs and clears every operating gate; Model B is the cleanest partner-operated pilot; exact consent phrasing, allocation between OAS and provider, retention periods and specific insurance/WSIB treatment depend on final facts and professional review.
- **Low confidence / unresolved:** any claim that a contract label alone shields OAS, that a generic “partners” disclosure is always sufficient, or that referral fees/backflow tasks have no additional restriction. Do not act on those assumptions.
